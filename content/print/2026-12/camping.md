Since July 1, sleeping or camping on land owned by the state or a city or county in Indiana has been against the law, unless that land has been set aside for it.

The law, Senate Bill 285, was signed by Gov. Mike Braun this spring. It makes camping on public property a Class C misdemeanor, which can mean up to 60 days in jail and a fine of up to $500, WLKY reported. [CHECK: the signing date and the effective date in the enacted text.]

## How it works

The law does not start with an arrest. When police first find someone camping on public property, WFYI reported, they are supposed to assess the person's mental health needs, give a warning and provide information about shelter and services. The version of the bill a Senate committee sent on in January also had officers offer a ride to a shelter or a place that provides services.

If the person is still camping there more than two days later, they can be charged. The law says no one can be convicted if there are no open shelter beds within five miles, according to WFYI, and it requires police to report these encounters so federal money for homeless programs can be better directed. A person who is charged can be sent to a diversion program. [CHECK: the five mile rule and the diversion language in the enacted text.]

The bill also bars a city or county from adopting a policy that discourages police from enforcing rules against camping or blocking sidewalks, and lets residents, business owners or the attorney general sue a local government that does. [CHECK: that this section survived in the enacted text.]

## Two views

Braun has said the law is meant to steer people toward help. "The purpose of the bill is not criminal action, but moving homeless Hoosiers toward social service networks to help them get needed services and stay safe," he said, according to WLKY. He told WFYI that Indiana's earlier housing first approach "has demonstrably not reduced homelessness."

Critics argue the law adds no new support and that criminal charges make it harder for people to get back on their feet. "You cannot legislate people out of homelessness without first investing in housing, mental health care, and addiction treatment," Pastor David Greene, who works on homelessness in Indianapolis, wrote in a statement quoted by WFYI.

## What Columbus is doing

Columbus started on this before the law did. The city formed its Encampment Task Force in March 2025, bringing together city and county departments, police, nonprofits and social service agencies to help people who are unhoused find stable, long term housing.

In April, the United Way of Bartholomew County named the task force its 2026 Advocate of the Year. According to the United Way, partners on the task force have rehoused 144 people and kept another 199 from losing their homes. The group's goal is what it calls functional zero, the point where more people are being housed than are becoming homeless.

"I like to think of what we're doing as enforcement with compassion," Mark Stewart, president of the United Way of Bartholomew County, said in the city's announcement. The task force pairs code enforcement, a police officer and the United Way to offer help at the same time.

Mayor Mary Ferdon said Columbus faces the same problems other cities do: homelessness, scarce money, a shortage of housing and workforce challenges. "But what sets us apart is the collaborative approach we take," she said.
