For decades, the stretch of the East Fork of the White River between the Second Street and Third Street bridges has been on the city's list of places that needed fixing. This fall, the work finally reached the water.

In September, crews began taking out the low head dam there, a structure more than 100 years old, according to Columbus Redevelopment. The city calls the dam's removal a priority for decades and says it will greatly improve both the health and the safety of that part of the river. [CHECK: Is the dam fully out? ## A project that changed shape

The city's project, called Our River... Our Riverfront, has been redrawn more than once. An earlier design included a rock riffle and a recreational channel, popular features whose cost ran past the budget. In 2025 a Guiding Team of stakeholders and community leaders helped Columbus Redevelopment redesign it, and the city decided to cut the scope and focus on safety and the environment.

The city lists five goals now: remove the low head dam, connect the riverfront trail, give emergency crews a way to the water, stabilize the banks and improve access for recreation. It also points to erosion at the dam that risked exposing a nearby delisted Superfund site, and to the awkward connections between popular stretches of the People Trail.

The state's summary of the project adds native plants, accessible overlooks, more green space, gathering areas and better lighting, with multi use trails tied into the paths the city already has.

## How it is being built

Work began on the west bank in early 2025, clearing the tree line for a staging area and access point while the redesign was finished and permits were amended. By the middle of 2025 the city had picked a contractor for the east bank, where the work includes armoring the bank, the trail connection and an amphitheater. ## Who is paying for it

The state's READI program lists the project at about $17.3 million: $5.6 million in READI funds, about $11.58 million in tax increment financing and $105,000 from private sources. Its project summary also names $1.72 million from the state's Next Level Trails program. The South Central Indiana region, which includes Bartholomew, Jackson and Jennings counties, received $30 million in READI money in all. ## What it is for

When it is done, the city says, the riverfront will strengthen the connection between downtown and Mill Race Park for residents and visitors alike, and give the river a new community gathering area with a new place to get into the water.
